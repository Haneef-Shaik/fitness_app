## USDA Table of Cooking Yields for Meat and Poultry, Release 2 (2014) - USDA ARS (also on data.gov / Ag Data Commons as CSV)
- URL: https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400535/Data/retn/USDA_CookingYields_MeatPoultry02.pdf
- Angle: Cooking transformation formulas and official factors (Q4)
- Quality: primary · published 2014-09
- Searcher note: Public USDA dataset for yield_factors.csv, covering meat, chicken and fish. Gives % cooking yield, % moisture change and % fat change by cut and cooking method (roast, grill, braise, pan-fry and so on) under set USDA protocols. It is published as a PDF plus CSV and data dictionary on data.gov and Ag Data Commons (https://data.nal.usda.gov/dataset/usda-table-cooking-yields-meat-and-poultry). These are the same moisture and fat change factors FNDDS applies at recipe level. Licence assumed public domain / CC0 like the sister retention-factor dataset; I could not open the Ag Data Commons page (HTTP 403) to confirm.

- [central] USDA defines cooking yield as hot cooked weight divided by raw weight, times 100. Wch is weighed while the sample is hot, after a very brief specified rest, and Wcr is the sample's weight before cooking. This gives a deterministic formula for converting raw weight to cooked weight.
  - quote: "The equation for calculating cooking yield is: Yield (%) = 100 x (Wch / Wcr) ... The cooked sample's hot cooked weight (Wch) is recorded after the sample has been cooked (while sample is hot, after a very brief specified resting time) using the specified cooking method."
- [central] Percent moisture change and percent fat change use the same formula: 100 x ((cooked nutrient content x cooked edible weight) - (raw nutrient content x raw edible weight)) / raw weight. Fat values replace water values for the fat version. A positive result means a gain and a negative result means a loss, so the table records fat lost or gained during cooking.
  - quote: "Moisture change (%) = 100 x ((Nc x Ec) – (Nr x Er)) / Wcr. The equation used for % Fat Change is the same as above, except that fat values are substituted for water values. This percent change for moisture or fat could be positive or negative, indicating a gain or loss, respectively."
- [supporting] USDA's Nutrient Data Laboratory calculates cooked-food nutrient values by applying cooking yields and/or retention factors to raw-ingredient values in recipes, and enters the results into SR. FNDDS also uses selected cooking yields. This is the official method behind the raw-to-cooked recipe calculation.
  - quote: "The Nutrient Data Laboratory (NDL) applies cooking yields and/or nutrient retention factors to food formulations and recipes to convert nutrient values for uncooked foods or ingredients into values for cooked foods. Those values are entered into the USDA National Nutrient Database for Standard Reference (SR). The Food Surveys Research Group uses select cooking yields for foods in the USDA Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS)."
- [supporting] The table covers meat and poultry only. In the ground-meat and sausage studies the meat was cooked with no added fat or oil, so these yields show the meat's own moisture and fat loss, not oil absorbed from frying. Frying-oil uptake has to come from a different source.
  - quote: "All ground beef patties were cooked to a final internal temperature of 160°F/71°C. No fat was added during cooking. ... Sausage patties were placed on an electric griddle preheated to 325°F/163°C and pan-fried without added oil according to package directions"
- [supporting] Each row gives the mean cooking yield % with n, SD, minimum and maximum yield %, so the data supports ranges as well as point values. Rows marked with a 1975 release year come from the older Agriculture Handbook 102 (AH-102). The numeric table itself is published separately in PDF/Excel; this PDF documents the methods only and contains no yield values.
  - quote: "n: Number of samples used in calculating values for this table. SD: Standard deviation of the mean; null if could not be calculated (i.e, n<3). Yield Minimum %: Minimum yield % in the data set. Yield Maximum %: Maximum yield % in the data set. ... Listings shown with a 1975 release year are based on data for items contained in AH-102 Food Yields"

## USDA Table of Nutrient Retention Factors, Release 6 (2007) - data.gov (PDF retn06.pdf + NutrientRetention.csv)
- URL: https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/usda-table-of-nutrient-retention-factors-release-6-2007
- Angle: Cooking transformation formulas and official factors (Q4)
- Quality: primary · published 2007-12 (data.gov metadata last updated 2025-11-22)
- Searcher note: Shippable source for retention. data.gov lists the licence as CC0 1.0 and access as public. Downloads are NutrientRetention.csv, a data dictionary and the PDF (https://www.ars.usda.gov/arsuserfiles/80400530/pdf/retn06.pdf). It covers 16 vitamins, 8 minerals and alcohol for about 290 foods, grouped by cooking method. The PDF gives the formulas verbatim: True Retention %TR = (Nc*Gc)/(Nr*Gr)*100. Nc and Nr are nutrient per g of cooked and raw food; Gc and Gr are the cooked and raw weights. Apparent Retention %AR = Nc(dry-weight basis)/Nr(dry-weight basis)*100 applies when weights are unknown. Covers vitamins and minerals only, not macronutrient or fat change.

- [central] The USDA Table of Nutrient Retention Factors, Release 6 comes from USDA ARS (Nutrient Data Laboratory). Its data.gov listing gives the licence as CC0 1.0 public domain. It is distributed as retn06.pdf, NutrientRetention.csv and a data dictionary CSV, with Figshare download links. This makes it shippable under FitLog's licensing constraint.
  - quote: "Publisher: Agricultural Research Service, Department of Agriculture ... License: https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/ ... retn06.pdf: https://ndownloader.figshare.com/files/44488751 ... NutrientRetention.csv: https://ndownloader.figshare.com/files/44488754"
- [central] USDA defines true retention (%TR) as %TR = (Nc*Gc)/(Nr*Gr)*100. Nc and Nr are the nutrient content per gram of cooked and raw food, and Gc and Gr are the cooked and raw weights. When weights are not available, it uses apparent retention on a dry-weight basis: %AR = Nc(dry wt)/Nr(dry wt)*100. So applying true retention needs the cooked-to-raw weight ratio (the yield). The retention factor alone is not enough.
  - quote: "Most retention factors were calculated by the True Retention Method (%TR). This method, as shown below, requires data on the weights of food before and after cooking, as well as the content of the nutrient of raw and cooked food (Murphy et al.1975). %TR = (Nc*Gc) / (Nr*Gr) * 100 ... If weights of food before and after cooking are unavailable, the retention factor can be calculated on a moisture-free basis, the Apparent Retention Method (%AR): %AR = [Nc (dry wt basis)] / [Nr (dry wt basis)] * 100"
- [central] The table covers only micronutrients and alcohol: 16 vitamins, 8 minerals and ethyl alcohol, about 26 components in all, for about 290 foods. It has no retention factors for energy, protein, fat or carbohydrate, and no yield or fat-absorption factors. Those have to come from other sources, such as the USDA yield tables. A check of NutrientRetention.csv found 26 nutrient IDs and 270 distinct retention codes. Sodium (Nutr_No 307) is one of them.
  - quote: "The table has retention factors for 16 vitamins, 8 minerals, and alcohol for approximately 290 foods. Nutrient retention factors are given for a range of cooking and preparation methods such as, but not limited to, baked, boiled, reheated, broiled, pared, and drained. Methods applied were based on food type."
- [supporting] The documentation shows a retention factor applied directly to a raw per-100 g value: raw spinach vitamin C 28 mg/100 g × 0.85 (boiled greens) = 23.8 mg per 100 g cooked spinach. The worked example makes no yield (weight-change) adjustment, even though the %TR definition depends on weight change.
  - quote: "the ascorbic acid content in raw spinach is 28mg/100g. The nutrient retention factor for boiled greens is 85%. ... (28mg ascorbic acid per 100g raw spinach) * 0.85 = 23.8 mg of ascorbic acid per 100g cooked spinach."
- [supporting] Factors are rounded to the nearest 5% and capped at 100%. Retention depends on cooking method and cooking time. For example, legumes boiled 45–75 minutes with the cooking water used (as in dal) keep 95% of sodium and 70% of thiamin. Drained, they keep 90% of sodium and 65% of thiamin. Boiled greens drained after cooking in water that covers them keep 55% of vitamin C. Stir-fried greens keep 85%.
  - quote: "The Nutrient Data Laboratory reports nutrient retention factors to the nearest 5 percent. Factors calculated above 100 percent are reported as 100 percent."

## Reinivuo H, Laitinen K. (2007) Proposal for the harmonisation of recipe calculation procedures. EuroFIR D2.2.12 (FAO-hosted)
- URL: https://www.fao.org/uploads/media/reinivuvo_laitinen_2007_Eurofir_recipe_harmonisation_D2.2.12_M2.2.4_02.pdf
- Angle: Cooking transformation formulas and official factors (Q4)
- Quality: primary · published 2007-04
- Searcher note: The EuroFIR recipe-calculation formulas, ready to implement in code. It compares the INFOODS, British, USDA retention-factor, EPIC, Bognar-Piekarski and yield-factor methods. Harmonised Procedure B: apply the yield factor at recipe level (to all ingredients equally) and retention factors to each ingredient. The 10-step USDA retention-factor method runs: grams, refuse, nutrients per ingredient, retention factors, raw weight sum, then % moisture change and % fat change at recipe level; cooked weight = raw + delta moisture + delta fat; nutrients per 100 g = totals / (cooked g / 100). Bognar rule for fried food: put in the recipe only the fat actually absorbed, not the fat used, and apply no retention factor to it. For cooked rice, pasta and legumes, leave the absorbed water out of the recipe. Licence of the report is unclear (EuroFIR deliverable), but the method itself is not copyrightable data.

- [central] The harmonised EuroFIR recipe calculation procedure applies the yield factor at recipe level and retention factors at ingredient level. Its formulas are: total cooked weight (g) = total raw weight (g) x yield factor; nutrient per 100 g cooked = (nutrient per 100 g of ingredient x raw weight of ingredient in g x retention factor) / total cooked weight in g. Worked example: cabbage at 30 ug/100 g, 1000 g raw, retention 0.70, cooked dish 2255 g gives 9 ug/100 g. Partners chose this procedure because it is the most commonly used, not because it was validated as the most accurate.
  - quote: "The EuroFIR recipe calculation procedure applies yield factor at recipe level and ingredient factors at ingredient level. [...] Total cooked weight g = total raw weight g * yield factor [...] Nutrient content per 100 g of cooked weight = nutrient content of ingredient * raw weight of ingredient g *retention factor / total cooked weight g [...] For example cabbage: 30 µg * 1000 g * 0.70 / 2255 g = 9 µg"
- [central] For fried and deep-fried foods, recipe calculation should enter only the fat actually absorbed as the cooking-fat ingredient, and no retention factor should be applied to fat uptake (Bognar and Piekarski method; Finnish database practice). In the Slovak databank, the fraction of frying fat counted as absorbed is left to the user, and 50% is often assumed.
  - quote: "the amount of cooking fat in the recipe of fried or deep fried food should be the same quantity as the fat absorbed. Retention factor for the fat uptake during frying or deep frying of a food should not be used. [...] if a food is fried or deep-fried (e.g. doughnut), only the absorbed amount of cooking fat is recorded as an ingredient. [...] Uptake of fat, for example, during frying of foods is optional and user can use proportion according to his or her needs. Often even 50% of fat is considered as an uptake during frying, so related amount of fat is included in ingredients."
- [central] In the retention factor method (Powers and Hoover 1989; Beecher and Matthews 1990), assigned percentages of moisture change and fat change are converted to grams. Energy and lipid are adjusted for the fat gained or lost. Cooked weight = total uncooked edible-portion weight + moisture change (g) + fat change (g), and nutrients per 100 g = recipe nutrient totals / (cooked weight / 100).
  - quote: "Using the assigned percentage of fat change, determine the grams of fat gained or lost from cooking, and adjust the energy content and lipid content to account for the gain or loss of fat in the cooked dish. 9. Add the change in moisture content in grams (step 7) and the change in fat content in grams to the total uncooked weight of the recipe (step 5) to determine the cooked weight of the recipe."
- [supporting] In the British (McCance and Widdowson) method, when weight change comes from water alone: nutrient per 100 g cooked dish = total nutrient in the raw ingredients / cooked dish weight x 100. If the cooked weight is unknown, it can be estimated from the percentage weight loss (or gain) of a similar dish. The British method cannot calculate fat change directly; such dishes need analysis.
  - quote: "The change in fat content cannot be calculated directly in the British method. [...] Nutrient content of cooked dish per 100 grams = total nutrient content of raw ingredients / weight of cooked dish x 100 [...] Weight of cooked dish = [Weight of raw ingredients x (100 - % weight loss of similar dish)]/100"
- [supporting] The choice between recipe-level and ingredient-level factors changes results only slightly: in one worked example, cooked weight was 2255 g vs 2405 g, and final nutrient X was 11.6, 12.6 or 11.9 ug/100 g under procedures a, b and c. The EuroFIR Compiler Network judged that calculated values differ more from analysed values than the calculation methods differ from each other, and that calculated values are only approximations.
  - quote: "It was also estimated that there is more difference between calculated and analysed values that between calculation methods."

## USDA ARS FNDDS 2019-2020 Documentation (Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies)
- URL: https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400530/pdf/fndds/2019_2020_FNDDS_Doc.pdf
- Angle: Cooking transformation formulas and official factors (Q4)
- Quality: primary · published 2022-10 (Issued October 2022; suggested citation year 2022)
- Searcher note: Describes the recipe calculation behind FNDDS, which FitLog's Indian dish records already use. Foods with several ingredient links are calculated with the retention-factor recipe method. A 'Moisture & Fat Adjustments' file holds recipe-level factors: moisture change as % of total item weight, plus fat gain or loss. Cooking yields are defined as weight change from evaporation or drip, water absorption (boiling) and fat gain or loss. Public domain (USDA). Directly reusable to keep FitLog's own dish calculations consistent with FNDDS.

- [central] FNDDS 2019-2020 derives nutrient profiles for about two-thirds of its 5,624 food/beverage codes by recipe calculation, using data from about 1,900 FoodData Central items. The recipe calculation has three steps: (1) choose ingredients and amounts, (2) apply nutrient retention and moisture adjustment factors where appropriate, (3) sum the ingredient nutrient values. The remaining third of codes are direct single-ingredient matches to one FDC code.
  - quote: "Data for about 1,900 items in FDC were used to determine the values for the 5624 food and beverage items in FNDDS 2019-2020. Approximately one-third of the FNDDS food codes are a direct match to a single FDC code... The nutrient profiles for the other two-thirds... were generated using a recipe calculation process utilizing two or more ingredients... Recipe calculation can be briefly summarized into 3 steps: select the ingredients and their amounts; apply factors, if appropriate – nutrient retention and moisture adjustment; sum nutrient values for all ingredients to generate a nutrient profile"
- [central] In FNDDS, moisture (water) change from cooking is a single percentage of the whole item's total weight and is applied at recipe level, not per ingredient. Ingredient weights are entered as pre-cooking weights. The chosen moisture loss comes from USDA 'Food Yields Summarized by Different Stages of Preparation' (1975), and some recipes were adjusted iteratively until their moisture matched a similar analysed food.
  - quote: "Provided in MoistAdjust, moisture change is expressed as a percentage of the total weight of the food/beverage item. Moisture loss is applied at the recipe-level; therefore, the weight for each ingredient is the weight before adjustments for moisture loss. Selection of a moisture loss to represent a cooked product is informed using USDA's Food Yields Summarized by Different Stages of Preparation (USDA, ARS, 1975) as well as other limited sources. For some recipes, moisture adjustments were performed until the moisture value in the recipe food was close to the moisture value of a similar analyzed food where available."
- [central] Since FNDDS 2015-2016, recipe calculations include no separate fat gain or fat loss factor for cooking. Cooking fat (for example, frying oil taken up) is entered as an explicit ingredient amount instead. Salt and fat used in preparation may be added as ingredients to represent the nutrient profile.
  - quote: "Any increase or decrease in fat during cooking is incorporated into the ingredients; therefore, recipe calculations do not include any fat change - gain or fat loss during cooking. This process began with FNDDS 2015-2016."
- [central] FNDDS takes cooking nutrient losses from the USDA Table of Nutrient Retention Factors, Release 6 (2007). That table covers only 16 vitamins, 8 minerals and alcohol; it has no macronutrient factors. Each factor is the percent of a nutrient retained after preparation, identified by a 4-digit food-group/cooking-method code and applied at ingredient level.
  - quote: "Nutrient losses that occur because of cooking are accounted for in many recipe calculations using the USDA Table of Nutrient Factors, Release 6 (USDA, ARS, NDL 2007). The table has retention factors for 16 vitamins, 8 minerals, and alcohol for types of foods; each retention factor is the percent of the specific nutrient that remains in the food after preparation... Each food group/cooking method (retention description) has a unique 4-digit retention code... the retention factor (percentages of nutrient retained) was applied at the ingredient-level to create the final nutrient profile."
- [supporting] FNDDS 2019-2020 has 22,710 portion weights (about 23,000), in grams of edible portion as consumed, for household units such as slice, piece, teaspoon and cup. These are generic composite estimates, and USDA warns they may not be valid for working out density (weight per volume). Every code also carries a 'Quantity Not Specified' default portion (code 90000), which USDA says should not be assumed to be the average amount consumed.
  - quote: "All weights are in grams of edible portion as consumed. Weights are estimations to represent a group of foods and beverages and may not account for all sizes available for a specific product... Portion weights in FNDDS... may not be applicable for calculating density or weight per volume for any specific liquid... Database users should not assume that QNS values accurately represent the average amount of a food or beverage consumed."

## Open-KNEAD: Knowledge-grounded Nutrition Estimation via Agentic Decomposition (Coburn, Yue, Ma, Raghavan, Vinod, Zhu; arXiv preprint, Jul 2026)
- URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12911
- Angle: Multi-step VLM pipeline and clarifying questions (Q6, Q9, Q10)
- Quality: primary · published 2026-07-14
- Searcher note: PREPRINT. This is the closest match to FitLog's Q10 multi-step design. An MLLM identifies each item, a selective retrieval step grounds it to USDA FNDDS by nutrient-aware matching, and a 'recipe-prior' step recovers energy added in cooking, especially for non-US cuisines. That step is the hidden-oil problem. It runs locally with no training. On the dietitian-verified ACETADA dataset the open-model agent's portion estimates beat the direct estimates of two frontier closed models by about 30% and 53%. The authors also say modern MLLMs' direct nutrient estimates now match or beat the full retrieval pipeline. They argue retrieval still earns its place through better portions and auditable item-by-item records. That supports putting the numbers in code and the database, not in the model. Related work from the same group: Coburn et al., IEEE BHI 2025 (arXiv 2507.07048). It reports that adding context metadata (venue type from GPS, meal type from timestamp, list of food items) cuts MAE and MAPE. That is useful evidence for a 'home vs restaurant' question.

- [central] For current multimodal LLMs, retrieval-augmented database grounding no longer improves the overall meal energy estimate: a modern MLLM's direct estimate matches or beats the full retrieval pipeline. On Nutrition5k with Gemma-MoE, the paper's Table 1 reports energy MAE of 121.9 kcal for direct prompting, 126.3 kcal for RAG weighted top-K and 111.2 kcal for RAG with a VLM pick, so results depend on the retrieval variant. This is a preprint and has not been peer reviewed.
  - quote: "Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) are increasingly used for dietary assessment from meal images, where retrieval-augmented grounding was shown to sharpen nutrition estimates. However, we find this premise no longer holds for current MLLMs. A modern MLLM's direct estimate now matches or surpasses the full retrieval pipeline."
- [central] Open-KNEAD is a training-free agentic pipeline that splits a meal into food items and grounds each one to a USDA FNDDS code through selective, nutrient-aware retrieval. The result is an auditable per-item record, and in most backbone and dataset settings it improves portion estimates over both earlier grounding methods and direct estimation. Examples with Gemma-MoE: portion MAE on Nutrition5k fell from 80.2 g (direct) to 66.7 g, and on OmniFood8K from 62.4 g to 43.5 g.
  - quote: "Each decomposed food item is grounded to a Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS) code via selective, nutrient-aware retrieval, composing an auditable per-item record. Across two open MLLM families and three cuisines, Open-KNEAD improves portion estimates over both prior grounding methods and direct estimation in most backbone-dataset settings."
- [central] A recipe-prior step inside the agent recovers cooking-added energy that the photo does not show, such as oil and fat. Without it, estimates for non-US cuisine are biased low. On OmniFood8K (Chinese cuisine, Gemma-MoE), adding the recipe prior moved the signed energy bias from -123.2 kcal to +37.3 kcal and cut energy MAE from 168.6 kcal to 139.1 kcal (Table 3).
  - quote: "An agent-internal recipe-prior step further recovers the invisible cooking-added energy that biases estimates on non-US cuisine."
- [supporting] On the dietitian-verified ACETADA dataset (Australian), the locally hosted open agent Gemma-31B reached a portion MAE of 147 g. That beat the direct portion estimates of GPT-5.5 (212 g, about 30% better) and Gemini (311 g, about 53% better). The closed models were tested only with direct estimation. For energy on ACETADA with Gemma-MoE, however, the agent scored worse than direct estimation (193.3 vs 163.5 kcal MAE).
  - quote: "on the dietitian-verified set, the strongest open model's local agent (Gemma-31B) reaches 147 g portion MAE, beating gpt-5.5's direct estimate (212 g, -30%) and Gemini's (311 g, -53%)"
- [supporting] Selective retrieval is capped by a per-meal budget of B=5 picks. On Nutrition5k the pipeline averaged 2.8 MLLM calls per meal, against 1.0 with no gating and 3.3 when it always picks (Table 4). That is the extra call cost of a multi-step design compared with a single call. The FNDDS knowledge base is released, but the paper gives no licence for the release. FNDDS itself is USDA public domain.
  - quote: "We release the Open-KNEAD framework and its agent-ready FNDDS knowledge base."

## Decomposing Food Images for Better Nutrition Analysis: A Nutritionist-Inspired Two-Step Multimodal LLM Approach (Khlaisamniang et al., CVPR 2025 Workshop MTF)
- URL: https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2025W/MTF/html/Khlaisamniang_Decomposing_Food_Images_for_Better_Nutrition_Analysis_A_Nutritionist-Inspired_Two-Step_CVPRW_2025_paper.html
- Angle: Multi-step VLM pipeline and clarifying questions (Q6, Q9, Q10)
- Quality: primary · published 2025-06 (CVPR 2025 Workshops, MTF; pp. 482-491)
- Searcher note: Peer-reviewed workshop paper. Step 1 has an off-the-shelf MLLM break the dish into its main ingredients, portion sizes and cooking details. Step 2 computes calories and macronutrients from that breakdown. It was more accurate than one-step direct queries on Nutrition320 (a Nutrition5k subset) and on Gindee121 (real-world app photos). It needed no fine-tuning or large ingredient database. This is evidence for the 'decompose then compute' split in Q10. It also suggests v2 schema fields for components with grams and a cooking method. Exact MAE figures need to be taken from the PDF, which could not be fetched.

- [central] The study tested a two-step prompting pipeline with no fine-tuning and no database. Step 1 has the MLLM output a JSON breakdown of the meal: ingredients, portion sizes, weight, edible percentage, oil used and cooking method. Step 2 estimates calories and macros from that JSON plus the raw image. For GPT-4o (2024-11-20), this cut calorie MAE from 89.305 to 78.489 kcal on Nutrition320 and from 97.554 to 85.512 kcal on Gindee121. Carb MAE fell from 10.205 to 8.220 g on Nutrition320 and fat MAE from 5.325 to 5.035 g. Caveats: Claude was not evaluated. Nutrition320 is a 10% subset of Nutrition5k with overhead images only and dishes of 1,000 kcal or less. Gindee121 is a Thai app set whose ground truth is user-edited values checked by a nutritionist, not weighed meals. Only MAE is reported, with no % error.
  - quote: "The food analysis prompt instructs the model to systematically extract key properties of a given meal, including ingredients, portion sizes, weight, edible percentage, oil used in food, and cooking method. Furthermore, we standardize the output format in JSON... [GPT-4o] On Nutrition320, it achieved the lowest calorie error (78.49 kcal, 12.11% better than baseline) and outperformed others in weight, carbs, and fat. On Gindee121, it improved calorie error by 10% (85.5 kcal)"
- [central] Decomposition does not help every model. On the full Nutrition5k set, two-step prompting made Qwen2.5-VL-72B worse: calorie MAE rose from 96.95 to 100.22 kcal, carb MAE from 10.21 to 12.10 g and fat MAE from 6.62 to 7.19 g. On Gindee121, two-step made Gemini-2.0-Flash worse: calorie MAE rose from 116.732 to 139.699 kcal and fat MAE from 10.609 to 12.348 g. It did help GPT-4o (88.86 to 80.32 kcal), Gemini-2.0-Flash on Nutrition5k (126.03 to 102.93 kcal) and Llama3.2-90B-Vision (118.90 to 106.26 kcal).
  - quote: "Two-Step Prompting outperforms the standard prompting in calorie estimation and macronutrient breakdown for GPT-4o, Gemini-2.0-Flash, and Llama3.2-Vision, while Qwen2.5-VL performs slightly worse than the baseline."
- [supporting] The image must be passed again in Step 2. When GPT-4o's Step 2 got only the text from Step 1, calorie MAE on Nutrition5k was 92.78 kcal. That is worse than the 80.32 kcal of two-step with the image, and also worse than the 88.86 kcal one-step baseline. Protein was the only nutrient that did not get worse without the image.
  - quote: "Step 2 was conducted without an input image to assess the impact of image presence in Step 2. This resulted in a significantly higher MAE score for both calorie and macronutrient estimation, except for protein"
- [supporting] Reasoning models gave mixed results. On Nutrition320, calorie MAE was 125.98 kcal for o1 (2024-12-17) and 139.98 kcal for Gemini-2.0-Flash-Thinking, against 89.31 kcal for GPT-4o and 108.48 kcal for Gemini-2.0-Flash. On Gindee121, o1 was best at 80.83 kcal against 97.55 for GPT-4o, while Gemini-2.0-Flash-Thinking (127.89) stayed worse than Gemini-2.0-Flash (112.84).
  - quote: "reasoning models perform worse than standard models. However, for the Gindee121 dataset, GPT-o1 outperforms all other models, while Gemini-2.0-Flash-Thinking still underperforms compared to Gemini-2.0-Flash."
- [tangential] The abstract calls visual prompting robust, but for GPT-4o on Nutrition320 every bounding-box and segmentation prompt did worse than the plain raw image. These were YOLO-World boxes, SAM2.1 masks and FoodSAM semantic and panoptic masks (Exps 01-08), with calorie MAE from 92.433 to 148.211 kcal against 89.305 kcal for the raw image. Food-level boxes were worst (148.211 kcal). Only Qwen2.5-VL on Gindee121 did best with bounding boxes (83.809 kcal).
  - quote: "Qwen2.5-VL: Bounding box prompting performed best for calorie estimation, exceeding GPT-4o Two-Step by 2% on Gindee121. [Table 2, GPT-4o, Nutrition320 Cal MAE: 00 = 89.305; 01 = 148.211; 02 = 123.133; 03 = 94.962; 04 = 92.433]"

## Bognar A. (2002) Tables on weight yield of food and retention factors of food constituents for the calculation of nutrient composition of cooked foods (dishes). BFE-R-02-03, Bundesforschungsanstalt fuer Ernaehrung, Karlsruhe
- URL: https://www.fao.org/uploads/media/bognar_bfe-r-02-03.pdf
- Angle: Cooking transformation formulas and official factors (Q4)
- Quality: primary · published 2002-10-14
- Searcher note: The main European source for cooking yields and fat uptake in frying. Part 1: weight-yield factors for about 700 foods and dishes, with formulas for whole dish, edible part, solid part and liquid part. Part 2: retention factors for protein, fat, carbohydrate, vitamins, minerals and salt across 39 food categories. It also gives fat uptake in g per 100 g of ready-to-cook ingredient for pan-frying and deep-frying (breaded and unbreaded meat, fish, vegetables, potato, mushrooms). Formula (8): fat uptake C(k,p) = D(k,p)*e(k,p) - D(k). D(k) = fat per 100 g of raw ingredients; D(k,p) = fat per 100 g of cooked dish; e(k,p) = weight-yield factor. Where no retention factor exists it recommends 1.0. LICENCE FLAG: the PDF has no copyright or licence statement, so commercial redistribution is unclear. - [central] Bognar defines the true-retention formula: retention factor a(j,k,p) = [Z(j,k,p) / X(j,k,p)] × e(k,p). Z is the nutrient per 100 g of cooked edible dish, X is the nutrient per 100 g of raw ingredients, and e is the edible-part weight yield factor, i.e. cooked weight divided by the total ready-to-cook ingredient weight U(k) without cooking water or frying fat. Appendix B then gives the recipe-calculation formula for nutrient j per 100 g of cooked dish: Q(j,k,p) = [Σ E(j,i) × x(i,k,p) / 100] × A(j,g,p). Here E(j,i) is the nutrient per 100 g of ingredient i, x(i,k,p) is the grams of ingredient i needed per 100 g of edible cooked dish, and A(j,g,p) is the category-average retention factor. ## DietAI24 as a framework for comprehensive nutrition estimation using multimodal large language models (Communications Medicine, 2025; PMC12589391)
- URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s43856-025-01159-0
- Angle: Multi-step VLM pipeline and clarifying questions (Q6, Q9, Q10)

- Searcher note: Peer-reviewed. The model describes the image, and those descriptions become several retrieval queries against a vector index of USDA FNDDS (5,624 foods, more than 23,000 portion sizes). The model then picks FNDDS food codes and portions, and nutrients (up to 65) come from the database, not from the model's own numbers. - [central] DietAI24 is a multi-step, zero-shot pipeline. GPT-4 or GPT-4 Turbo Vision describes the image, then retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) over an FNDDS 2019-2020 vector index (OpenAI text-embedding-3-large) finds candidate food codes, then the model infers codes and portions. - [central] Portion size is treated as multiclass classification over FNDDS standard household-portion descriptors (e.g. '1 roll', '3 cups').   - quote: "the high Abs Diff. in nutrients like folate (25.67) and phosphorus (1.55) suggests inaccuracies in the original GPT Vision model's estimations, which might indicate that the original GPT Vision model has learned some invalid knowledge or experienced hallucination."

## Thames et al. (2021), Nutrition5k: Towards Automatic Nutritional Understanding of Generic Food, CVPR 2021
- URL: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.03375
- Angle: Portion size: reference objects, depth and density (Q3)
- Quality: primary · published 2021-03-04 (arXiv v1); v2 revised 2021-06-22; published at CVPR 2021
- Searcher note: This is the clearest published measure of how much depth sensing helps, and all figures come from one dataset: Nutri-Test, 5,000 real cafeteria dishes weighed per ingredient and valued with USDA data. Each result is calorie MAE as a percentage of the mean. RGB image with direct regression: 26.1% for calories, 31.9% aggregate for macronutrients. Adding overhead depth (RGB-D): 18.8% for calories, 20.9% for macros. Image plus a depth-derived volume to estimate mass, then a per-gram model: 16.5% for calories, 26.2% for macros. The per-gram model alone scores 9.5%, but only when the true mass is supplied. The authors write that portion estimation roughly triples the error, since the MAE goes from 9.5% to 26.1%. They also claim the model beat 4 nutritionists and 16 amateurs who were given scale hints (a coin weighs 5.6 g, a phone 113 g). Capture was 4 side-angle videos plus an overhead Intel RealSense RGB-D image. Evidence: Moderate (benchmark). The dataset is on GitHub and I believe it is CC BY 4.0, but I did not confirm this, so check before use.

- [central] On the Nutri-Test overhead RGB-D subset, adding a depth-derived food volume scalar (from an Intel RealSense D435 camera at 35.9 cm, summed per-pixel over segmented food) to an image-only mass regressor cut total mass MAE from 38.1 g (29.5%) to 29.4 g (13.7%). Combining this with the per-gram model gave an end-to-end calorie MAE of 41.3 kcal (16.5%), against 70.6 kcal (26.1%) for 2D direct prediction. Caveat: the paper's own mass figures don't agree across tables. The image-only mass MAE is 29.5% in Table 4 but 18.8% for 2D direct in Table 3, and the analysis text says '18.7% to 13.7%'.
  - quote: "Table 4. Mean absolute error for mass regression from an RGB (and optionally volume estimate) input. ... Image-only 38.1g / 29.5% ... Image+volume 29.4g / 13.7% ... an end to end direct nutrition prediction with a calorie MAE of 16.5% and aggregate macronutrient MAE of 26.2%"
- [central] Portion estimation is the main source of error. A model that predicts calories per gram (multiplied by the ground-truth mass) reached a calorie MAE of 24.1 kcal (9.5%). Predicting absolute calories directly from one RGB image reached 70.6 kcal (26.1%), roughly 3x the error. So recognising the food and its energy density is much easier than estimating how much of it there is.
  - quote: "Predicting direct calories compared to calories per gram increases the MAE nearly 3x from 9.5% to 26.1%. This demonstrates the substantial increase in complexity of the challenge when portion estimation is required."
- [supporting] Fat is the hardest macronutrient to predict from a 2D image alone. 2D direct prediction gave fat MAE 5.0 g (34.2%), carbs 6.1 g (31.9%) and protein 5.5 g (29.5%). Adding depth as a 4th input channel brought fat MAE down to 2.27 g (18.1%). A baseline that always predicts the mean gave fat MAE 8.2 g (67.6%).
  - quote: "2D Direct Prediction 70.6 / 26.1% 40.4 / 18.8% 5.0 / 34.2% 6.1 / 31.9% 5.5 / 29.5% ... Depth as 4th Channel 47.6 / 18.8% 40.7 / 18.9% 2.27 / 18.1% 4.6 / 23.8% 3.7 / 20.9%"
- [supporting] In a small survey, 16 non-nutritionists and 4 nutritionists visually estimated ingredient mass on 10 deliberately simple plates, given reference points (a US quarter weighs 5.6 g, a phone 113 g). Their average percent error in total dish mass was 53% for the non-nutritionists and 41% for the nutritionists. The authors say their 2D model outperforms both groups. A further 32 non-nutritionists estimated volume instead of mass and were slightly less accurate.
  - quote: "The average percent error for non-nutritionists is 53%, which is consistent with previous reports of high human inaccuracy when performing visual portion size estimation [10, 18]. The nutritionists had a lower average percent error rate of 41%."
- [supporting] Nutrition5k has about 5,000 unique dishes (5,066 per Table 1) and 20k short videos, built from more than 250 ingredients, averaging 5.7 ingredients per plate. 3.5k dishes also have overhead RGB-D images. Ground truth comes from weighing each item as it is added (scale precision +/-1 g) and computing nutrients from recipes via USDA FNDDS. All data comes from one cafeteria and is mostly Western food, so it is not a good benchmark for Indian dishes. The paper does not state the dataset licence; check it on the GitHub repo.
  - quote: "Nutrition5k contains 20k short videos generated from roughly 5000 unique dishes constructed from more than 250 different ingredients. Each dish has a full breakdown of ingredient labels, their quantities, and their macronutrient information computed using the USDA Food and Nutrient Database ... the limited geographic nature of the collection inherently skews the food seen to mostly western style dishes."

- Searcher note: This study measures the user-effort cost of a reference card plus two photos. goFOOD (Lu et al. 2020, Nutrients; PMC7436102) needs 2 photos from different angles, with a credit-card-sized fiducial card in frame, before and after eating. This is a direct cost of reference-object and multi-photo portion methods.
  - quote: "Of the 468 recordings of nonpackaged food items captured by the app, 60 (12.8%) had to be discarded due to errors in the capturing procedure."
- [central] Using the fiducial marker (a credit-card-sized reference card) was the largest single error category (19 of 60 discarded recordings). Plate problems accounted for 8, combinations of errors for 17, and other causes such as a hand blocking the camera or unmatched recording pairs for 16.
  - quote: "The principal problems were as follows: wrong fiducial marker or improper marker use (19 recordings), plate issues such as a noncompatible or nonvisible plate (8 recordings), a combination of various issues (17 recordings), and other reasons such as obstacles (hand) in front of the camera or matching recording pairs (16 recordings)."
- [supporting] Even after face-to-face instruction, written instructions and a demo video, just over half of participants (52%, 25/48) made at least one capture mistake. Errors were not associated with age, sex, technical knowledge or time since instruction (chi-square tests).
  - quote: "Our analysis of the errors revealed that 52% (25/48) participants made at least one error, whereas 4 participants made 4 errors and 1 participant made 6 errors."
- [supporting] The goFOOD Lite volume-estimation protocol needs 2 images per recording taken from different angles (0 and 15 degrees), plus a credit-card-sized reference card that is fully visible, lies on the same surface and does not move between shots. The whole plate must also be in frame, and elliptical white or high-contrast plates work best. Non-elliptical, patterned, transparent or reflective plates were a problem for the algorithm.
  - quote: "As in other apps, goFOOD Lite uses a specially designed reference card as a fiducial marker that must be placed next to the recorded item to ensure that the 3D volume estimation is accurate."
- [supporting] Citing other studies, the authors report that users prefer a credit-card-sized fiducial marker (preferred by 91% of adults and 67% of adolescents in Daugherty et al.). They suggest that phones with 2 or more rear cameras could remove the need for a marker, and they recommend exploring alternatives to fiducial markers.
  - quote: "However, with the new generation of smartphones equipped with 2 or more rear cameras, the fiducial marker can be eliminated, as 2 images can be captured simultaneously [ 19 , 27 ]."
