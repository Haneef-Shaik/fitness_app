## 2) Indian gym-goers and market size

All figures in this section come from Deloitte India with the Health & Fitness Association (HFA), Sep 2025 [S51][S52]. They are **[ESTIMATE]**: modelled from interviews and surveys.

**Members and gyms**
- 12.3M gym/studio members in 2024, which is **0.8% penetration** of adults. Forecast: 23.2M (1.7%) by 2030.
- 46,500 facilities, rising to 65,500.
- Revenue about ₹16,200 Cr ($1.9B), rising to ₹37,700 Cr by 2030 (15% a year).
- [ANALYSIS] That works out to about ₹13,200 per member per year, and about 265 members per facility on average.

**Segments**
- **Value gyms**:
  - fees range from under ₹8,000 to ₹14,000 a year;
  - 80% of facilities (about 37,200), 78% of members (9.6M), 56% of revenue;
  - spaces of 500–3,000 sq ft;
  - many "offer 1–2 weeks PT" free.
  - [ANALYSIS] About 258 members per value gym. This is the long tail a free gym-software play would target.
- **Premium**: from ₹14,000 to ₹50,000+ a year; 12% of centres, 18% of members, 38% of revenue.
- **Boutique**: about ₹19,000–30,000 a year; fastest-growing at about 19% a year.

**Geography and Tier-2/3**
- The top 10 cities (Mumbai, Delhi-NCR, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Chennai, Pune, Jaipur, Lucknow, Kolkata, Kochi) have 31% of centres (14,500), 41% of members (5.1M) and 56% of revenue.
- The **rest of India has 32,000 centres and 7.1M members**. Deloitte says it "remains largely untapped" and "lack[s] adequate fitness infrastructure".

**Funnel**
- Of 956M Indians aged 18–62, 138M are physically active. Only 21M pay for it:
  - 12M at gyms or studios;
  - 3M on paid online fitness;
  - 6M on sports.
- About 819M are inactive. Women are more inactive than men.

**Who joins, why, and why they quit**
- People aged 28–35 dominate: 54% of them pay for fitness.
- First-time joiners cite appearance (54%), health (26%) and social influence (14%).
- **About 32% of those who leave do so for a "temporary break" and an equal 32% to "switch to home workouts". About 48% of dropouts would rejoin.**
- Among non-members, 52% cite affordability and 50% don't want to change their routine.
- 50% of facilities offer group classes.
- The report names retention as "a major challenge".

**How people track workouts and diet**
- [USER FEEDBACK, 2021] "it's really difficult to track calorie intake with Indian food… apps… are wildly inaccurate" [S53].
- [USER FEEDBACK] A Healthify user said a coach's "diet chart like someone downloaded from chat gpt", and another that "coaches… just share YouTube videos" [S37].
- WhatsApp diet charts, notebooks or PDFs, and Instagram coaches: **no quantified source found**. Fittr's WhatsApp origin [S42] and its 700 coaches [S41] are indirect evidence that tracking happens in chat and is coach-led.
- Supplement culture: **not found**.

**Gen Z**
- Strava/TechCrunch describe a global trend of young people using running clubs to socialise [S46]. There is no India-specific gym data for Gen Z.
- Deloitte's core paying cohort is 28–35, not Gen Z [S52].

## 5) What's commoditized vs underserved — [ANALYSIS]

### Commoditized or crowded
1. **Multi-gym access passes.** Cult (708 centres, about 987k members) dominates the metros. FITPASS is smaller but profitable and corporate-led. Fitternity was absorbed and impaired. Gyms resent the low per-visit payouts.
2. **AI photo and text calorie logging.** Healthify (Snap, multilingual Ria, Indian database), MyFitnessPal Meal Scan, wearable apps and many small startups all offer it, so the feature alone won't differentiate.
3. **Home workout video** (cultpass HOME) and free wearable companion apps (NoiseFit).
4. **Generic diet charts from coaches.** Users already see these as low value.

### Underserved, and relevant to the free-gym-software plan
1. **About 37,000 independent value gyms.** They hold 80% of facilities, average about 258 members, charge under ₹14k a year, and 32,000 of them sit outside the top 10 cities.
   - Cult's partner software ("digitised centre operations… CRM") is tied to joining its network.
   - Cult itself relies on third-party point-of-sale systems [S4][S12].
   - There is no independent, free, member-facing digital layer for these gyms.
   - Because owners are wary of aggregators, a pitch of "we don't take your members, we help you keep them" stands apart from Cult and FITPASS.
2. **Retention.** The industry's main pain is that 32% of leavers move to home workouts and 48% of dropouts would rejoin [S52].
   - An app that keeps logs, PRs and diet going at home, and visible to the gym, addresses both the owner's churn and the member's continuity.
   - Cult's own retention is only about 51% [S5].
3. **The trainer–member workflow.** Value gyms give 1–2 weeks of free PT, and personal training is Cult's fastest-growing line at 73% a year.
   - Trainers today appear to work through chat or PDFs (WhatsApp evidence is anecdotal).
   - Structured plan delivery, logging and progress views are a gap.
4. **Pricing people trust.** No-refund and auto-renew complaints run across Cult, FITPASS and Healthify. Transparent monthly pricing with an easy cancel is a positioning opportunity.
5. **Accurate Indian home-food logging.** It is still contested: MyFitnessPal is inconsistent, and Healthify is strong but gated behind payment and sales calls.

### Economics of the channel
- India has low willingness to pay: a 0.3x price index and about $0.06–0.09 revenue per install in 14 days.
- Paid installs for Health & Fitness cost about ₹31 at a 15.9% signup rate, so one paid signup costs roughly ₹200 (derived).
- One average gym of about 265 members is therefore roughly ₹50k of equivalent paid-acquisition value, if most members activate. That is an upper bound.
- Monetisation is likely to come from gym-side add-ons, coaching or PT, or B2B rather than from members' app subscriptions. Healthify's pivot to GLP-1 and the US is a warning about consumer-only subscriptions in India.

### Risks
- Cult could bundle free software for its 490 franchise and marketplace gyms.
- Gyms may block an app they see as a route to poaching members.
- Data from Tier-2/3 cities is thin.
