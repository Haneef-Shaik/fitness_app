## 1) Case by case

### Fresha (salon and spa software, free core)
- **Model:** Free booking, POS and calendar software. Fresha earns from payment processing, a one-off fee on marketplace-sourced new clients, and paid add-ons. Its pricing page as served to us (India version) lists the "Independent" and "Team" plans as Free, with paid add-ons such as Client Loyalty at ₹5,000/location/month and pay-per-SMS. It describes the marketplace fee as a "one-time fee applied to brand new clients who discover your business via the Fresha marketplace", and returning clients carry no fee. [COMPANY CLAIM, S5, accessed 2 Oct 2026]
- **New-client fee level:** 20% with a $6 minimum. [UNVERIFIED, competitor blog S6, accessed 2 Oct 2026]
- **Is it still free?** Competitor blogs say Fresha dropped its free-forever plan in 2025, with roughly $19.95/month solo and $14.95 per team member per month. [UNVERIFIED, S6] This conflicts with Fresha's own page, which shows Free plans but also says "as you expand we charge for each bookable team member". [COMPANY CLAIM, S5] Pricing probably differs by market; this is unresolved.
- **Scale and funding:** $80M from KKR at a valuation above $1B (22 May 2026), $285M raised in total. The company reports 130,000+ businesses, 35M+ appointments a month, about $15B annual GMV, and says it is "already profitable". [FACT for funding; COMPANY CLAIM for metrics, S1] Founded 2015. [FACT, S1]
- **How big the marketplace really is (key data point):** The marketplace produced 3M new client-to-business connections in 2025 and accounts for "nearly 10% of total new client acquisition". 63% of marketplace-acquired clients rebook within 12 months. [COMPANY CLAIM, S3, 24 Feb 2026]
  - On 30–35M appointments a month (about 400M a year), 3M marketplace connections is under 1% of appointments. [ESTIMATE]
  - So roughly 90% of new clients, and nearly all appointments, still come from the salon's own channels. The software is the business; the consumer network is a thin layer on top.
- **Consumer app:** The app passed 1M downloads a month, up 300% since mid-2024. CEO William Zeqiri: "Booking is no longer a one-off action; it's becoming part of a routine." [COMPANY CLAIM, S4, 24 Apr 2026]
- **What failed:** Merchants dispute who "found" the client.
  - "Fresha has charged me marketplace fees for customers who found my business through Google." [USER FEEDBACK, S7, review dated 30 Sep 2026]
  - "Fresha still refused to return its £55 fee." [USER FEEDBACK, S7]
- **Lesson for gyms:** Ten years and $285M of capital produced a profitable company, but the consumer marketplace is a small minority of demand. If you monetise "your" members and get the attribution wrong, you destroy merchant trust.

### Booksy, Treatwell, Vagaro
- **Booksy:**
  - Boost is a "one-time 30% commission… of the total cost of the first visit, up to a maximum of $100"; later visits carry no commission. Booksy claims "44 million clients worldwide book on Booksy". [COMPANY CLAIM, S8, accessed 2 Oct 2026]
  - **This conflict maps directly onto gym QR check-ins:**
    - "People who literally walk into my shop, scan our QR code, and book… are still counted as Boost." [USER FEEDBACK, S9, 16 Aug 2025]
    - "The app will charge you for boost even when you directly refer your customers to download the app." [USER FEEDBACK, S9, 28 Jul 2025]
  - Revenue figures for Booksy (for example "$114.9M in 2025") appear only on template and estimate sites. [UNVERIFIED, not used]
- **Treatwell (formerly Wahanda, owned by Recruit):** "35% commission on first bookings made by new clients we bring you from the marketplace", "0% commission for repeat bookings", 2.5% fee on online prepayment. Bookings made directly by phone, social or the merchant's website are commission-free. [COMPANY CLAIM, S10, accessed 2 Oct 2026]
- **Vagaro:** Paid SaaS, not free. It reached a $1B valuation with FTV Capital on 2 Nov 2021. [FACT, S11] FTV's case study is titled "How Embedding Payments into Vertical Software Led to Outsized Growth" (24 May 2023); I could not retrieve the body. [FACT for title, S11] Per-seat pricing of about $30/month is [UNVERIFIED, third-party].
- **Lesson:** The salon industry has settled on a norm: the platform charges only for demand it demonstrably creates, and charges nothing on the merchant's own and repeat clients. Even so, attribution disputes are the main source of merchant anger.

### Mindbody + ClassPass (fitness: the closest analog)
- **Mindbody's software plus app:**
  - The app has "3M+ active users".
  - Mindbody businesses take "more than 60M classes and appointments each month".
  - Only "more than 22,000 consumers per month discover and make their first purchase at a business through the Mindbody app". [COMPANY CLAIM, S12, accessed 2 Oct 2026]
  - Monthly marketplace first purchases are about 0.04% of monthly bookings. [ESTIMATE] After roughly two decades of studio software, the cross-studio consumer marketplace is marginal to studio demand.
- **Marketplace fee:** About 20% on marketplace new clients, capped at $30. Operators complain about being charged on self-sourced clients. [UNVERIFIED, third-party review aggregator S13] Mindbody's own page says only that "certain fees or commissions may apply". [COMPANY CLAIM, S12]
- **Payments as the engine:** In 2020, a16z put Mindbody at about $250 per customer per month: about $150 for software plus about $100 from payments. [FACT as reported by a16z, S14, 4 Aug 2020]
- **ClassPass:**
  - Mindbody acquired it in Oct 2021 in an all-stock deal, alongside a $500M investment led by Sixth Street. [FACT, S19, 13 Oct 2021]
  - It has paid partners $3.1B to date, and "94% of ClassPass users are new to the venues they visit". [COMPANY CLAIM, S15, 26 Feb 2026]
  - ClassPass pays studios "40 to 90% of the standard retail price depending on demand". [COMPANY CLAIM via S16, accessed]
- **Studio conflict:**
  - In 2015 the NYT called ClassPass a "middleman" and described a "power imbalance". [FACT, cited in S18]
  - Some owners cap ClassPass spots "to prevent being cannibalized". [FACT, S18]
  - An Association of Fitness Studios study found average conversion of ClassPass visitors into direct studio customers of about 6%, usually under 10% and as low as 2%. [FACT as reported, S17, accessed]
  - CEO Fritz Lanman: "Stronger businesses do better on ClassPass, and weaker businesses do worse." [COMPANY CLAIM, S15]
  - Mindbody now offers a 90-day guarantee to pay studios "twice the difference" if revenue is not incremental. [COMPANY CLAIM, S15] That guarantee shows how much trust the conflict has cost.
- **Lesson:** Owning both the gym's software and a cross-gym consumer pass creates a structural channel conflict. Mindbody had to manage it with caps, dynamic pricing and guarantees.

### Playtomic, Hudle, Playo (sports venues)
- **Playtomic:** A player app plus Playtomic Manager club software.
  - Raised €65M (€55M equity plus €10M Santander debt), reported 21 Mar 2025.
  - Reports 6,000+ clubs, 3M players, 63 countries, and "€240M+" 2024 gross revenue growing about 40%. [FACT for funding; COMPANY CLAIM for metrics, S20]
  - "Gross revenue" is most likely gross booking value, not net revenue. [ESTIMATE]
  - It bought venue software to seed supply: Playven (Jan 2020; the headline also claims over 1M users). [COMPANY CLAIM, S22]
  - A competitor says clubs pay €59–€279/month plus 4% of booking revenue, and players pay a service fee. [UNVERIFIED, S21]
- **Why it works:** Padel needs four players. The app solves a real cross-player, multi-venue coordination problem (finding matches and partners), which gives strong local network effects. The consumer has a reason to multi-home across clubs.
- **Hudle (India):** $2.5M Series A (21 Jul 2025), 2,000+ venues in 60 cities, 150,000 games a month, 3.5x GTV growth and 2.5x revenue growth year on year. [FACT/COMPANY CLAIM, S23] The model is player booking, not venue software. [S23]
- **Playo:** The site lists Indian and Gulf cities but gives no user or venue counts and no venue software. [S24] Numbers: not found.
- **Lesson:** Venue software as a wedge works when consumers need many venues and other players. Indian sports-venue players remain small after years in market.

### Practo (India) and Doctolib (France): failure-to-pivot vs. success
- **Practo:**
  - Started with Ray clinic SaaS in 2008, added consumer search and booking, then teleconsult, pharmacy, diagnostics and insurance. [FACT, S26, 8 Oct 2018]
  - By 2018: 130,000 doctors, 80,000 clinics, $234M raised, five acquisitions, and staff cut from 2,000+ to about 900. Doctors doubted lead-gen value, citing a "shuffle" between providers driven by commissions. [FACT/USER FEEDBACK, S26]
  - Secondary analysis: small clinics "balked" at ₹999/month SaaS fees, and the 2020 round was at about half the 2017 valuation. [UNVERIFIED, S27]
  - FY25: revenue ₹234 Cr (FY24: ₹240 Cr), operating EBITDA ₹15 Cr, the first full profitable year, 5L+ doctors listed, IPO prep. [COMPANY CLAIM, S25, 5 Aug 2025]
  - That is about 17 years to profitability, with revenue still small. [ESTIMATE]
  - **Lesson:** Indian practitioners resisted paying, and the consumer side diversified away from the SaaS core. Doctor software did not become a strong consumer moat.
- **Doctolib:**
  - Practitioners pay €139/month for booking, €135 for the medical-record software, €79 for teleconsultation. "Patients don't pay." [FACT/COMPANY CLAIM, S28, accessed]
  - 2024: revenue €348M (+22.5%), operating loss €53.8M, about 400,000 professionals. Germany is about 25% of users and 20% of revenue. [FACT, S29, 2 May 2025]
  - France: 62% of Île-de-France residents use online booking platforms, and 79% of those use Doctolib (Mar 2019). [FACT, S31] Back in 2016, only 2–4% of practitioners used online booking. [FACT, S32]
  - **Merchant-to-consumer conversion data:** In Germany, 400M+ appointments have been managed in Doctolib software since launch, but only 60M were booked by patients themselves. [FACT, S30, 1 Jul 2024] That is about 15% patient self-booking. [ESTIMATE]
  - **Lesson:** The best case charged practitioners real money from day one because the software saved staff time. Consumer adoption followed over years, helped by a national event (COVID vaccine booking, not verified here).

### Indian free and freemium SMB software
- **Khatabook:** FY24 revenue ₹102.7 Cr against a net loss of ₹116.2 Cr; expenses ₹230 Cr. [FACT, S33, Nov 2024] Laid off about 42 staff, roughly 6% of 700, "in line with profitability goals". [FACT, S34, accessed]
- **OkCredit:**
  - FY25 operating revenue ₹23.3 Cr, net loss ₹23.2 Cr, claimed 100M cumulative downloads. [FACT/COMPANY CLAIM, S35, 24 Oct 2025]
  - It earlier cut about 40 staff (30–35%) to refocus on fintech. [FACT, S36]
  - FY21 revenue was reportedly about ₹3.8 lakh on about ₹115 Cr spend. [UNVERIFIED, Entrackr via search]
- **Vyapar (freemium billing):** FY25 revenue ₹69 Cr (+53%), loss ₹63 Cr, about 90% of revenue from licence sales, and cash down 93%. [FACT, S37, 28 Nov 2025]
- **Petpooja (paid restaurant POS):** FY24 revenue ₹77.2 Cr with a ₹13.4 Cr loss; FY25 revenue ₹102.3 Cr with a ₹21.4 Cr loss. [FACT, S38, accessed] It is under tax-authority scrutiny over alleged POS sales manipulation by restaurants. [FACT, S39]
- **Dukaan:** Cut about 30% of staff on a pivot "from SMBs to D2C brands" (15 Mar 2023). [FACT, S40] Headcount fell from about 220 to under 70. [FACT, S41, 2 Aug 2023]
- **Bikayi:** Laid off about 90% of staff and pivoted to Bik.ai. About 95% of revenue growth was tied to counterfeit-seller transactions. [FACT as reported, S42, 5 Aug 2022] A Bikayi executive: "about 50 million SMBs in India but not more than 20,000 that can run online stores." [S41]
- **Lesson:**
  - Free tools reach huge install bases (tens of millions of downloads) but earn tens of crores of revenue and lose a similar amount.
  - The money in India came from mission-critical paid workflow (Petpooja) or licences (Vyapar), not from free usage turned into a network.

## 2) Cross-case patterns: when free SaaS turns into a network, and when it doesn't

**Where the consumer network became real** (OpenTable, Doctolib, Fresha, Playtomic), three conditions held:
1. **The consumer is multi-merchant.** People see several doctors, try new restaurants and salons, and play padel at many clubs with changing partners. The consumer app solves a problem that no single merchant can solve. [ANALYSIS from S3, S20, S30, S46]
2. **Booking or payment runs through the software**, so the merchant's workflow pushes consumers onto the platform. [ANALYSIS]
3. **Demand is dense locally** (cities, clubs): the "atomic network" condition. [S52]

**Where it stayed weak or caused conflict:**
- **Single-home relationships.** A member mostly uses one gym or studio. Cross-merchant discovery then becomes a channel competing with the merchant (ClassPass, Mindbody marketplace). [S15–S18]
- **Monetising the consumer side by taxing the merchant's "own" customers.** Misattribution complaints recur at Fresha and Booksy, and in Mindbody's third-party reviews. [S7, S9, S13]
- **SMB segments too poor or informal to pay.** India's free ledger and store apps all show this. [S33–S42]
- **The consumer side diversifying away from the merchant core** (Practo). [S26, S27]

**Scale reality:** Even in the success cases, marketplace-originated demand is a small minority: Fresha about 10% of new clients, under 1% of appointments; Mindbody about 22k first purchases a month against 60M bookings; Doctolib Germany about 15% patient self-booking. [ESTIMATES on S3, S12, S30] The software P&L carries these companies for years.

**Time and capital:**

| Company | Founded | Capital / losses | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresha | 2015 | $285M raised | Claims profitable in 2026 |
| Doctolib | 2013 | €53.8M operating loss in 2024 | Not yet profitable |
| Practo | 2008 | — | First profitable year FY25 |

[S1, S25, S29]

**Free versus paid:** The two cleanest network outcomes, OpenTable and Doctolib, charged merchants meaningful subscriptions. "Free" is a distribution tactic, not a precondition, and it tends to erode later (Fresha's disputed shift to paid plans). [S5, S6, S28, S46]

## 4) Evidence on merchant-invited consumer adoption

- **PureGym (UK, 2013):** Over 40% of the membership base, 110,581 unique users, downloaded the myPureGym app within six months. The app was the way to book and cancel classes. [UNVERIFIED: DMA award text via search snippet; page returned 404 on fetch, S54]
- **Total Fitness (UK, 2017):** 12,000+ of 94,000 members, about 13%, downloaded the Netpulse-built app in the first month. 80% of members bring a phone into the club. [FACT, S53, 16 Jun 2017]
- **Doctolib Germany:** About 15% of appointments managed in the software were self-booked by patients (60M of 400M+, cumulative since launch). [ESTIMATE on S30] Online bookings grew 25% year on year (H1 2024). [S30]
- **Doctolib France:** 62% of Île-de-France residents use booking platforms after about 6 years. [S31]
- **Mindbody:** 3M+ app users against the studios' member bases; 22k marketplace-originated first purchases a month. [S12]
- **Fresha:** 1M app downloads a month at 140k businesses. [S4]
- **ClassPass to studio conversion:** about 6% (the reverse direction). [S17]
- **Not found:** Published "percent of a merchant's customers who install the platform app after an invite" figures for Fresha, Booksy, Practo or Playtomic.

**Inferred base rate [ANALYSIS]:**
- About 10–15% early adoption when the app is optional.
- About 40%+ when it is the required interface (booking, check-in, payment).
- Patient self-service converges to about 15–60% over years only with strong habit triggers.

## 5) Theory and its critiques

- **Dixon (31 Jan 2015):** "The tool helps get to initial critical mass. The network creates the long term value… and defensibility." Single-player tools are "kindling". [S48]
  - Caveat: his examples, Delicious and Instagram, are cases where the *same user* uses the tool and then the network.
  - In B2B2C the tool user (gym owner) is not the network user (member). That is closer to OpenTable's "network of reservation books" than to Dixon's pattern. [ANALYSIS; S46]
- **Bodnick (1 Dec 2016):** Great networks have social function "from the start". He lists failures that bolted networks onto big tool bases: Google Buzz, Apple Ping, Microsoft. [S49]
- **Casado & Lauten, a16z (9 May 2019):** "There generally isn't an inherent network effect that comes from merely having more data." Data moats "erode as the data corpus grows". [S50] This undercuts the idea that "workout data from many gyms produces a moat".
- **NFX (Jun 2021):** "Data nfx tend to be weaker than many people — particularly venture capitalists — often want to believe."
  - Marketplace network effects are asymptotic.
  - "Market networks" pair SaaS workflow with a many-sided network, which is the most relevant category for gyms, trainers and members. [S51]
- **Andrew Chen (9 Dec 2021):** The atomic network is "the smallest network needed that can stand on its own." "Your product's first atomic network is probably smaller and more specific than you think." [S52] For gyms, the atomic unit is plausibly *one gym's members and trainers*, not a city.
- **a16z (4 Aug 2020):** Fintech is the scaling engine of vertical SaaS (2–5x). [S14] The critique is implicit in its own caveat that it needs more than $50M GMV, and in India's payment economics.

## 6) Direct implications for the gym strategy [ANALYSIS: my interpretation, not sourced fact]

1. **Expect the consumer network to be a minority add-on for years, not the engine.**
   - Even Fresha (about 10% of new clients) and Mindbody (about 22k marketplace first purchases a month) show cross-merchant demand is thin.
   - Underwrite the business on gym-side economics first, and treat member-network revenue as optionality.
2. **Gym membership is single-home, which weakens the cross-gym network effect.**
   - Unlike padel, doctors or salons, a member rarely needs many gyms.
   - Cross-gym utility comes mainly from ClassPass-style passes, and those cannibalise and anger owners (6% conversion, capped spots).
   - Build the **atomic network inside each gym** (member–trainer–member: challenges, attendance streaks, coach programmes, leaderboards). Treat cross-gym features as later and opt-in.
3. **Make the app mandatory for workflow, not optional by invitation.**
   - Adoption evidence: about 13% in month one when optional (Total Fitness); over 40% in six months when the app handles class booking (PureGym, unverified).
   - Route check-in (QR), fee payment and renewal, class and PT booking, and diet or workout plans through the member app.
   - Track activation at the gym level: % of members active at 30 and 90 days.
4. **Write the attribution rules down in the contract before launch.**
   - Members brought or enrolled by the gym, including via the gym's QR code, should never carry platform fees and never be marketed rival gyms.
   - Booksy's QR misattribution complaints describe exactly the failure mode a gym QR check-in creates.
   - Copy Treatwell's terms (0% on repeat and merchant-sourced clients) and add an auditable source log.
   - Indian merchant groups organise collectively against platforms (Zomato Gold: about 2,000 restaurants logged out).
5. **Don't count on Toast-style payments income in India until UPI economics are verified.**
   - If UPI merchant fees are near zero, the 2–5x fintech uplift shrinks.
   - Alternatives: working-capital lending to gyms secured on membership receivables, EMI/BNPL on annual memberships (financing income), card and EMI fees, and a consumer premium tier (AI coaching or nutrition).
   - Khatabook and OkCredit show lending and payments revenue scaling slowly against burn.
6. **Free is a distribution tactic. Plan the paid tier from day one.**
   - The cleanest network successes (OpenTable, Doctolib) charged merchants. Fresha's "free" appears to be eroding.
   - In India, Petpooja shows paid vertical software can reach about ₹100 Cr when it is mission-critical (billing, tax, aggregator integration).
   - For gyms the mission-critical job is **collections and renewals plus attendance**. Monetise that, for example with a fee on automated renewals or a premium tier.
   - Free-only Indian SMB apps (Khatabook, OkCredit, Dukaan, Bikayi) mostly failed to monetise.
7. **Budget for a long runway.** Fresha took about 11 years and $285M to profitability, Doctolib is still loss-making after 11 years, and Practo took about 17 years. Gym-side unit economics need to be self-funding early.
8. **Data is not the moat.** Per Casado and NFX, aggregated workout data is a weak network effect. Defensibility comes from switching costs (member records, payment mandates, trainer workflows) and from in-gym social density.
9. **Suggested kill and scale metrics:**
   - Member app activation above 40% at 90 days in pilot gyms.
   - Share of gym revenue collected through the platform.
   - Member weekly active rate.
   - Gym churn below SaaS benchmarks.
   - Share of members who perform any cross-gym or cross-member action. If this stays under about 5%, the "network" thesis is unproven.
